| 3-minute draft | 7-minute final |

## Slide 1 — Title (0:00 – 0:25) · 25 seconds

> Good [morning / afternoon]. I'm [your name], applying for **Communications Director**. [pause]
>
> Two weeks ago I gave you a three-minute draft of this talk. You gave me thoughtful feedback. Today's seven-minute version is what I built from it — a fuller view of the data, a more concrete strategy, and a clearer plan for how I'd measure whether it's working.

**Coaching note:** Acknowledging the feedback loop builds trust. It says: "I listened. I revised. I came back stronger." That's a Communications Director skill on display.

---

## Slide 2 — Two neighborhoods, one city (0:25 – 1:05) · 40 seconds

> Two neighborhoods, both in Clear City. [pause]
>
> **The Dunes** — twelve hundred residents. Working-class, younger, more multilingual, with serious health needs. [pause]
>
> **Palm Haven** — eight hundred residents. Mid-to-upper income, digitally connected, civically engaged. [pause]
>
> Same city. Same services on offer. **Profoundly different starting points.** That means our communications cannot be one-size-fits-all.

**Coaching note:** This slide didn't exist in the draft. It's here so the audience meets the neighborhoods *before* the data — short character sketches first, numbers second. People remember stories. Numbers reinforce stories.

---

## Slide 3 — Five things I looked at (1:05 – 1:55) · 50 seconds

> Five things I looked at — health, age, language, and the two ends of the income spectrum. [pause]
>
> I picked these because each one changes how we should communicate. [pause]
>
> Health drives what we **lead with**. Age affects how we **talk to families**. Language affects whether we **get through at all**. And income shapes which **channels reach people**. [pause]
>
> You can read each pair on its own here, but the more interesting view is what happens when we put them in conversation.

**Coaching note:** This slide is your first chart, and it's the friendliest one — a pair of sticks per metric, each one labeled. You're earning the audience's trust before you ask them to read a more complex chart on the next slide.

The sentence "*each one changes how we should communicate*" is the bridge between data and strategy. It tells the audience: I'm not showing you facts for their own sake.

---

## Slide 4 — Same data, tilted view (1:55 – 2:50) · 55 seconds

> Now look at the same data tilted ninety degrees. [pause]
>
> Each line connects the same metric across the two neighborhoods — left side is The Dunes, right side is Palm Haven. The steeper the line, the bigger the gap. [pause]
>
> Four of the five lines slope **downward** — The Dunes higher than Palm Haven on health risk, children, Spanish at home, and lower income. [pause]
>
> One line goes the **other way** — higher income, where Palm Haven is far ahead. [pause]
>
> The steepest line — and the most consequential one — is **the health gap**.

**Coaching note:** Saying "tilted ninety degrees" is a kind gift to your audience. You're acknowledging that the same data can look different — and giving them permission to take a moment to re-orient. That's good communication design without naming it.

---

## Slide 5 — The headline (2:50 – 3:50) · 60 seconds

> **Fifty-nine percent** of Dunes residents are at elevated weight risk. **Twenty-three percent** in Palm Haven. A **thirty-six-point gap**. [long pause — three full seconds]
>
> For context: a thirty-six-point gap is the difference between a public-health emergency and a healthy community. [pause]
>
> So if I'm running communications for both neighborhoods, what does this mean in practice? [pause]
>
> In Palm Haven, health is **one campaign a year** — a winter wellness push, maybe. [pause]
>
> In The Dunes, health is **the through-line of everything we publish**. Every newsletter, every event, every billboard touches it somehow.

**Coaching note:** This is the emotional center of the talk. Don't rush. The "one campaign a year" / "through-line of everything we publish" contrast is the entire point of the deck — that two neighborhoods don't get the same content calendar. Land it.

---

## Slide 6 — Why income demands different channels (3:50 – 4:45) · 55 seconds

> Income flips the script. [pause]
>
> **Sixty-nine percent** of Dunes households earn under twenty-five thousand a year. **Forty-five percent** of Palm Haven households earn over forty thousand. [pause]
>
> That gap matters because it shapes **which channels actually reach people**. [pause]
>
> Households in The Dunes are less likely to have unlimited data, a home computer, or the time to read a long email at lunch. So **print, text message, and in-person aren't fallbacks — they're the front door**. [pause]
>
> Households in Palm Haven have all of those things. **Email and self-serve portals aren't shortcuts — they're respectful** of how this community actually wants to engage.

**Coaching note:** "Aren't fallbacks — they're the front door" and "aren't shortcuts — they're respectful" are the two sentences worth memorizing on this slide. They reframe channel choice as **respect for the audience**, not as cost-cutting. That matters in a city government context.

---

## Slide 7 — Two strategies (4:45 – 6:00) · 75 seconds

> So what does this mean for Clear City? **Two strategies, not one.** [pause]
>
> For **The Dunes** — Spanish first. Written natively, not translated as an afterthought. Plain language. Health is the through-line. The schools, the faith communities, and community health workers carry the message — they're the trusted messengers, not us. Print, text, and in-person. **Same message, three formats — text it, print it, hand it out.** [pause]
>
> For **Palm Haven** — digital-first works. Captioned video, screen-reader friendly, keyboard-accessible. Lead with civic engagement. Self-serve, transparent, data-rich. And two-way — comment forms, surveys, public meetings. **They want to engage with us, not just receive us.**

**Coaching note:** This is the slide that earns the role. You're showing the panel that you can synthesize data into actionable strategy. Practice this one most.

The line "they want to engage with us, not just receive us" is a small but important nuance — it shows you understand that civic communications is two-way, not just broadcast.

---

## Slide 8 — 90 days + how I'll measure (6:00 – 7:00) · 60 seconds

> Sequenced. Measurable. Honest. [pause]
>
> **First thirty days — audit.** I don't translate anything, change anything, or launch anything until I understand what's already in market. [pause]
>
> **Next thirty — build.** A Spanish-language editor. An accessibility checklist on every asset before it ships. [pause]
>
> **Final thirty — re-mix.** SMS for The Dunes. A clean email program for Palm Haven. Sunset what isn't working. [pause]
>
> And here's how I'll know it's working — **reach by neighborhood, never aggregate**, because aggregate dashboards hide the gap. **Outcome metrics** tied to program goals. **An accessibility scan** on every published asset. And **short trust surveys**, before and after every event, so we know if we're earning trust or just claiming it.

**Coaching note:** Adding the measurement section is the biggest substantive change from the draft. City officials want to know how they'll judge whether this is working. "Aggregate dashboards hide the gap" is the kind of sentence that signals you understand the *political* side of segmented data, not just the analytical side.

## Anticipated Q&A — short answers

**"How would you partner with our other departments?"**
The audit comes first. Public Health, Parks & Rec, Library, Schools — each already knows these residents in ways I don't. Month one is listening to them, not pitching to them. Communications sits on top of their work, not in front of it.

**"What if a department disagrees with the strategy?"**
Communications is a service to the rest of the city, not a referee. If Public Health says my health framing is off, I update it. If Schools say SMS won't work for parents, I find out why and pilot something else. Disagreement is data.

**"What's the budget assumption?"**
The 90-day plan is mostly a re-allocation, not a net add. The audit shows what's not working — that's the budget for what is. Texts are cheap. A part-time Spanish-language editor goes a long way.

**"What would you ship in week one?"**
Nothing. Week one I'm listening. The fastest way to lose a community's trust is to show up with a press release before you've shown up at a town hall.

**"How do you handle a communications crisis with these constraints?"**
Crisis communications is the moment our two strategies converge — same urgent message, same accuracy, but two delivery vehicles. Spanish text + English text, same minute. Print follows within 24 hours. Crisis doesn't mean shortcut on accessibility — it means accessibility matters more, not less.

**"How do you measure trust?"**
Short, anonymous surveys at every event — before and after. Three questions: "Did you understand what we shared?" "Was it useful?" "Do you trust the city more or less than before this event?" The third question is the only one that matters.
